Gun Bench

The five gun portraits, drawn by the repo's own code — shared/glow.js and drawCannonPortrait, lifted verbatim. Change the elevation here before it changes in the game. The railgun keeps its own offset from the slider — it is a flat-trajectory gun.

True brightness: dark fills under additive glow, on the board's own ground.

The mount did most of it

It was a splayed trapezoid — a mortar baseplate. Now a cruciform of outriggers on screw-jack pads, with a pedestal and traversing race. Shared by all five types, as the mount always was.

The breech sits behind the trunnion

A gun does not pivot at the back of its own barrel. The breech swings down as the muzzle comes up — the clearest cue that the gun is elevating rather than merely pointing.

The tube tapers

A parallel-sided rectangle was most of the pipe impression. It tapers to a reinforced muzzle band now, and the three evenly spaced reinforcing bands drop to one. Evenly spaced bands read as a ladder.

Three mounts, not a boolean

Towed outriggers for the field guns · deck plate and drum for the railgun · bunker casemate for the autocannon. What a gun stands on is read first, because it is the widest thing on the card. Started as a boolean and did not survive the second one.

Four barrels, not six

Only the front arc of a rotary cluster is visible side-on; all six merged into one fat tube at this size. Four are drawn as tubes, each with its own collar and bore. Showing what can be seen beats modelling what is there.

The railgun is emplaced, not towed

A bolted deck plate and turret drum instead of outriggers, an armoured housing instead of an open carriage, and a flat trajectory — it is a direct-fire weapon, so it does not sit at flak elevation. The mount is now per type, because which one a gun stands on is as much of its identity as the barrel.

No tube, on purpose

A railgun accelerates its round down a pair of rails in open air. Drawing a solid barrel around them would be drawing the one thing the weapon does not have — so the frame is open, and the coil ladder running its length is what makes it read as an accelerator rather than a girder.

Coils on the top, plate on the side

A second pair of rails offset back and up gives the assembly a top face and a near face. The coils are exposed along the top channel; the flank is closed. Running the ladder down both faces made it read as open lattice from every angle — which is a ladder, not a machine.

The trunnion sits high on the mount

A towed gun's trunnion sits on top of its carriage; a turret's sits high, with the fixed armour below the rails rather than wrapped around them. Pivoting at the mount's centre is what kept burying it behind the counterweight.

One projection for the whole gun

Housing, counterweight, collar and muzzle brace all take the same depth offset as the rails — back and up. They were built with the generic back-face offset, which points down-right, so two halves of one rigid body looked seen from opposite sides. A depth cue only works if every part agrees on it.

Five parts, named apart

Base plate bolted down · mount traversing on the drum · housing elevating with the gun · counterweight behind it · rails out the front. Calling the mount "the housing" is what hid the fact that the housing was missing.

Why 60° and not 70°

Past about 65° the equilibrator, cradle and breech fold in behind the tube and the gun loses the triangle of linkage that reads as artillery. Drag the slider past 70 and watch it happen.

Silhouette exposure lifts the gamma hard — deliberately unfaithful, and useful because shape is the only thing it shows. It is how this art gets checked at all: a headless canvas renders the additive glow far dimmer than a phone does, so a faithful capture is unreadable and an is there ink here test answers yes wherever it is pointed.
